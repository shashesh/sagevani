Design principle

The original SageVani system had pillars, rivers, formats, seeds, and many named series. It was imaginative, but too much architecture can cause a writer to classify an insight before fully hearing it.

Foundation v1 uses the smallest structure that preserves identity.

Four spaces, four human questions

Dharma — How shall I live?

Possible territory:

work and responsibility;

relationships and speech;

ethics and consequence;

ambition and right action;

money and desire;

family roles;

difficult choices;

karma yoga;

social responsibility;

duty vs alignment.

Adhyātma — Who am I?

Possible territory:

Ātman and jīva;

witness and mind;

identity;

ahaṃkāra;

fear and death;

awareness;

bondage and mokṣa;

Vedāntic disagreement;

neti neti;

Bhakti — What is the Divine, and what is my relationship to it?

Possible territory:

Krishna, Shiva, Devi, Rama, Hanuman and other forms of the Divine;

prayer;

grace;

surrender;

longing;

līlā;

sacred story;

saints and devotees;

Possible territory:

Cross-cutting lenses

“Modern Maya,” “Seeker's Mind,” “Myth & Symbol,” and “Sanātana Glossary” no longer need to be formal Rivers. Keep them as optional lenses in the Vani Vault.

Other useful lenses may emerge:

modern technology and attention;

family life;

work;

migration/diaspora;

grief;

beauty and ritual;

philosophical disagreement;

language and translation.

A lens exists to help a piece. A piece does not exist to populate a lens.

Formats

Do not over-standardize formats yet. For now, recognize four natural shapes:

Vani Note

100–350 words. One clear noticing. No attempt to exhaust a subject.

Inquiry Essay

700–1,800 words. Begins with a real question and follows it through text, thought, and life.

Text / Story Study

A verse, passage, character, episode, hymn, or symbol examined carefully with source context and interpretation clearly marked.

Practice Journal

A lived experiment in japa, meditation, silence, study, ritual, seva, or another practice. Honest enough to include boredom, inconsistency, doubt, and failure.

These are descriptions, not quotas.

One concept worth protecting: “Gita in the Grocery Store”

The name can change, but the idea should survive: test sacred philosophy in ordinary life.

Examples:

What does karma yoga look like when someone else gets credit for your work?

What does vairāgya look like when you desperately want a particular outcome?

What does ahiṃsā look like in the way you speak to yourself?

What does aparigraha look like when buying is frictionless?

What happens to “I am not the body” when somebody insults your appearance?

What does surrender mean when a family decision still has to be made by Friday?

This is one of SageVani's most distinctive territories: ancient thought encountering Tuesday afternoon.
