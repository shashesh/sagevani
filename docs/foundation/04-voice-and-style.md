# 04 — Voice & Style Guide

> Foundation handbook v1.0 · October 2026 · Source transcription, not a revision.
> Paragraph text is preserved; Word layout and typography are not reproduced.

The SageVani voice in one line

Intimate enough to feel lived, rigorous enough to be trusted, spacious enough to leave the reader with a question.

Core qualities

Seeker, not guru

Write from encounter rather than superiority.

Prefer:

“I have been sitting with this verse because it unsettles my idea of detachment.”

Avoid:

“Most people misunderstand detachment. Here is the truth.”

Warm, not sentimental

Tenderness is welcome. Manufactured profundity is not.

Avoid making every paragraph end like a quote card.

Poetic, but clear

A metaphor should reveal something, not fog it.

If a sentence sounds beautiful but becomes difficult to paraphrase plainly, test whether it is actually saying anything.

Devotional, but grounded

It is acceptable to write directly to Krishna, Devi, Shiva, Rama, or the Divine. It is also acceptable to say, “I do not know what to make of this.” Devotion does not require pretending certainty.

Curious, not relativistic

SageVani can acknowledge many interpretations without implying that all interpretations are equally well supported.

Preferred movement of an essay

Many strong SageVani pieces will naturally move like this:

ordinary moment → question → text/story → context → interpretation(s) → inward turn → lived implication → open ending

The form should remain flexible. The movement matters more than the template.

Sentence-level habits

Use:

concrete moments;

first-person honesty;

precise verbs;

short Sanskrit explanations;

questions that genuinely complicate the subject;

enough context for a reader who is not already steeped in the tradition;

occasional silence on the page — short paragraphs when earned.

Use sparingly:

“ancient wisdom”;

“higher vibration”;

“the universe wants…”;

“everything happens for a reason”;

“ego death” as casual shorthand;

“science proves…”;

absolute claims about “Hinduism”;

moralizing the reader's suffering;

therapeutic language when the piece is not actually grounded in psychology.

Phrases that should trigger a source check

“The Vedas say…”

“According to Sanātana Dharma…”

“Our ancestors knew…”

“This deity represents…”

“The ten heads of Rāvaṇa symbolize…”

“Quantum physics proves…”

“This mantra changes your vibration…”

“The Sanskrit literally means…”

“For thousands of years…”

None of these phrases is automatically false. They are simply places where writers often become careless.

The SageVani “turn inward”

A piece should not stop at explaining a concept.

After discussing dharma, ask where obligation and desire collide in your own life.

After discussing māyā, notice where an identity is being defended.

After discussing bhakti, ask what love looks like when no consolation arrives.

After discussing vairāgya, notice what outcome you are bargaining with reality to receive.

The inward turn is where SageVani stops being a summary website.

Titles

Titles should promise a real inquiry, not manufacture certainty.

Prefer:

What Does Surrender Look Like on a Tuesday?

Kunti's Impossible Prayer

When Detachment Starts to Look Like Avoidance

Māyā in the Age of the Curated Self

Why Does Arjuna Have to Act?

Avoid:

7 Ancient Secrets That Will Change Your Life

The Hidden Hindu Truth Nobody Tells You

Master Detachment in 5 Steps

Sanskrit style

For the serious long-form site:

use standard diacritics at first reference when practical;

immediately give a natural English gloss;

do not italicize every Sanskrit term repeatedly;

do not assume one English equivalent exhausts the word;

distinguish a lexical meaning from a philosophical interpretation.

Example:

Vairāgya is often translated as dispassion or non-attachment. But the word becomes more interesting when we ask what kind of freedom from craving a particular text or teacher has in view.

Endings

SageVani does not need to wrap every spiritual question in a bow.

Strong endings may be:

a question worth carrying;

a return to the opening ordinary moment;

a brief practice;

a verse seen differently after the exploration;

an admission of what remains unresolved.

The reader should leave with resonance, not a motivational slogan.
