# 07 — Writing & Publishing Workflow

> Foundation handbook v1.0 · October 2026 · Source transcription, not a revision.
> Paragraph text is preserved; Word layout and typography are not reproduced.

The principle

Do not begin with “I need a post.” Begin with “Something here will not leave me alone.”

Stage 1 — Notice

Capture the seed without explaining it.

Examples:

“Why did being misunderstood bother me for three days?”

“Kunti's prayer seems almost impossible to want.”

“I say I want detachment, but I also want the result.”

Write the Seed Card. Stop if that is all the thought has earned today.

Stage 2 — Sit with it

Before research, spend a little time with your own question.

Ask:

What do I already believe about this?

Why do I want that belief to be true?

What experience is underneath the question?

Am I trying to teach something I have not yet confronted?

This protects the piece from becoming a stitched-together research summary.

Stage 3 — Research

Only now begin gathering sources.

For a scriptural piece:

1. locate the passage;

2. read the surrounding context;

3. compare at least two translations when wording matters;

4. identify at least one traditional commentary when interpretation matters;

5. look for disagreement if the claim sounds universal;

6. consult reputable academic work for historical or comparative claims;

7. create a Source Card.

Research should complicate your first thought at least once. If every source merely confirms your initial conclusion, check whether you are searching selectively.

Stage 4 — Find the real question again

After research, ask:

What is this piece actually about now?

Often it will have changed.

A piece that began as “Māyā means misidentification” may become “Why are there so many different things hiding inside our English word illusion?”

That change is not failure. It is the work becoming more honest.

Stage 5 — Draft from life, not notes

Close most of the research tabs.

Draft in your own voice. Reopen sources when you need to quote, verify, or represent an interpretation precisely.

A useful loose shape:

1. ordinary human moment;

2. question;

3. scripture/story/term;

4. necessary context;

5. one or more interpretations;

6. inward turn;

7. lived implication;

8. an ending that remains open enough to breathe.

Stage 6 — Integrity pass

Use the checklist in 03 — Editorial Integrity & Research Standard.

Mark each major sentence mentally as:

T — textual/source claim;

Tr — traditional interpretation;

R — reflection;

P — practice/invitation.

If you cannot tell what kind of sentence it is, the reader probably cannot either.

Stage 7 — Voice pass

Read aloud.

Remove:

unnecessary Sanskrit;

sentences written to sound profound;

repeated metaphors;

preachy “you should” statements;

fake certainty;

conclusions that arrive too neatly;

generic opening paragraphs that could belong to any wellness blog.

Keep the sentence that feels slightly vulnerable but true.

Stage 8 — Quiet test

Put the piece away briefly if possible. Return and ask:

Does it still feel true without the emotional high of writing it?

Did I make the tradition smaller in order to make the article cleaner?

Am I hiding behind research instead of saying what is personally at stake?

Am I hiding behind personal experience instead of doing the required research?

Stage 9 — Publication

SageVani will not publish to satisfy an arbitrary quota in its foundation stage.

A piece is publishable when:

the question is alive;

the sources are sound enough for the claims;

the distinction between text, tradition, and reflection is clear;

the writing has an inward turn;

the voice sounds like SageVani rather than generic spiritual content;

there is no known unresolved factual problem that materially affects the piece.

Stage 10 — Release

After publishing, release ownership.

Do not use engagement as the final measure of spiritual or intellectual worth.

Return to the central movement:

Silence → Listening → Seeing → Speaking → Serving → Silence.
