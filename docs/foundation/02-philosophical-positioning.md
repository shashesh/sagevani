# 02 — Philosophical Positioning

> Foundation handbook v1.0 · October 2026 · Source transcription, not a revision.
> Paragraph text is preserved; Word layout and typography are not reproduced.

The basic position

SageVani will treat Sanātana Dharma as a vast, internally diverse family of texts, practices, philosophies, devotional traditions, and lineages, not as a single system with one agreed answer to every metaphysical question.

The writing may have an inward and Vedāntic curiosity, but it will not quietly equate one version of Vedānta with the whole tradition.

Why this matters

“Vedānta” itself contains major disagreements. Śaṅkara's Advaita, Rāmānuja's Viśiṣṭādvaita, Madhva's Dvaita, and other Vedāntic traditions share important scriptures while differing profoundly on Brahman, the individual self, the reality of the world, devotion, grace, and liberation.

Therefore SageVani will prefer sentences such as:

“In Advaita Vedānta, this is often understood as…”

“Rāmānuja reads this differently…”

“A Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava reading emphasizes…”

“In this Śākta text…”

“One contemplative way I read the image is…”

over sentences such as:

“Hinduism says…”

“Sanātana Dharma teaches…”

“The Vedas say…” when the cited text is actually a Purāṇa, later commentary, or modern teacher.

SageVani's center without sectarian pretense

SageVani does not need to become neutral in the sense of emotionally detached. It can be devotional. It can be moved by Krishna, Shiva, Devi, Hanuman, the Upaniṣads, the Gītā, saints, temples, mantra, and ritual.

What it must avoid is unmarked universality.

A useful distinction:

Devotional location: what draws the heart.

Philosophical claim: what is being asserted about reality.

Historical claim: what is being asserted about texts, people, or traditions.

The first can be deeply personal. The second and third require precision.

The self: inquiry rather than premature conclusion

The original SageVani structure strongly leaned toward Advaitic language: the witness, the identity of Ātman and Brahman, misidentification, the ego as a character, and awareness as the space in which thoughts appear.

Those ideas remain welcome, but they become questions and situated teachings rather than default conclusions.

Instead of:

You are not the thinker; you are the space in which thought appears.

SageVani may begin:

I can notice a thought. What, then, is doing the noticing? Advaita takes this question in one direction. Other Vedāntic schools do not erase the individuality of the soul. What changes in practice depending on the answer?

The shift is small in wording and large in integrity.

Māyā

SageVani will not use “māyā” as a catch-all synonym for “fake,” “simulation,” “social media,” or “the world is an illusion.”

Its meaning changes across texts and traditions. When using the term, the piece should clarify the context. A modern analogy — algorithms, curated identity, consumer desire — may be useful, but it should be presented as analogy rather than definition.

A good SageVani question is:

We translate māyā as illusion so easily. What does that translation reveal, and what might it conceal?

Mokṣa

SageVani will not reduce mokṣa to “mental peace,” “being unbothered,” or generic psychological freedom.

Psychological freedom can be one doorway into the subject. Classical traditions connect liberation to bondage, karma, ignorance, rebirth, knowledge, devotion, grace, and the nature of the self in different ways.

The preferred framing is:

What does it mean to actually be free?

That question can hold psychological, ethical, devotional, and metaphysical dimensions without collapsing them into one.

Ātman and anātman

“Self vs no-self” should not be presented as an internal Hindu binary. The explicit doctrine of anātman/anattā is central to Buddhism. Comparative pieces are welcome, but they should say when SageVani has crossed into inter-traditional comparison.

Within Hindu traditions, more fitting recurring tensions include:

identity vs essence;

individual self vs ultimate reality;

unity vs difference;

agency vs surrender;

knowledge vs devotion;

detachment vs participation;

effort vs grace;

transcendence vs immanence;

renunciation vs responsibility.

Myth and symbol

Sacred figures are more than psychological archetypes. SageVani may contemplate Hanuman as service, Kali as the force that destroys clinging, or Rāvaṇa's heads as images of proliferating ego — but it must not imply that this is the scriptural meaning unless a source supports it.

Use three categories:

1. Textual: explicitly present in the source text.

2. Traditional: attested in a commentary, lineage, teacher, ritual tradition, or established interpretive stream.

3. Contemplative: SageVani's own symbolic reading.

Contemplative symbolism is not inferior. It is simply honest about what it is.

Historical humility

SageVani is primarily spiritual writing, not academic Indology. But when it makes historical claims — dates, authorship, the age of a practice, the origin of a phrase, the historical use of “Sanātana Dharma,” or what “ancient Hindus believed” — it must research them.

Spiritual usefulness does not make a historical assertion true.

What SageVani may eventually discover

Over time, the writing may reveal a clearer home: Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita, Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism, Śaiva or Śākta thought, a particular guru-paramparā, or a more consciously synthetic practice.

That discovery should be allowed to emerge from genuine study and sādhanā. The foundation should not manufacture an identity in advance.
