# 03 — Editorial Integrity & Research Standard

> Foundation handbook v1.0 · October 2026 · Source transcription, not a revision.
> Paragraph text is preserved; Word layout and typography are not reproduced.

The central rule

Never let a beautiful sentence become more authoritative than the evidence behind it.

SageVani can be poetic. It can be intimate. It can take interpretive risks. But the reader should always be able to tell what kind of statement is being made.

The four layers of a SageVani piece

Not every article must visibly use these headings, but the writer should know which layer each important claim belongs to.

1. Text — What does the source actually say?

Record:

text name;

chapter/canto/book;

verse or passage number;

Sanskrit when useful;

translation and translator/edition;

immediate narrative or philosophical context.

Do not quote a floating verse without checking what comes before and after it.

2. Tradition — How has it been interpreted?

When interpretation matters, identify the source:

classical commentator;

sampradāya or philosophical school;

teacher or lineage;

modern scholar.

If commentators disagree, disagreement is part of the story, not a problem to hide.

3. Reflection — What does it awaken in me?

This is SageVani's contemplative voice.

Use language such as:

“I read this as…”

“One way this lands for me…”

“I wonder whether…”

“This image makes me notice…”

“In my own life…”

Personal insight does not need to impersonate scripture to be valuable.

4. Practice — What might be lived or tested?

Offer practices as invitations, not guarantees.

Prefer:

“You might sit with this question for a week…”

rather than:

“Do this for seven days and your anxiety will disappear.”

Source hierarchy

Tier 1 — Primary text

Use the Sanskrit text or a dependable edition whenever possible. Record exact location.

Tier 2 — Classical/traditional commentary

Use commentary to understand how a school or lineage reads the text. Attribute it by name rather than calling it “the Hindu meaning.”

Tier 3 — Reputable academic secondary source

Use for history, philosophy, philology, comparisons among schools, textual questions, and contested claims. Prefer university publications, scholarly encyclopedias, and serious monographs.

Tier 4 — Living-tradition teaching

Discourses, books, ashram publications, sampradāya websites, temple resources, and contemporary teachers are valuable for understanding living practice. Label the tradition or teacher clearly.

Tier 5 — General web / social media

Use as a lead, never as the final authority for a serious claim. Trace quotations and symbolic claims back to better sources.

The “quote attributed to…” rule

If a quotation is widely attributed to Krishna, Vivekananda, Ramana Maharshi, the Buddha, Rumi, Kabir, or another figure but a reliable source cannot be found, SageVani does not present it as authenticated.

Options:

omit it;

label it “often attributed to…”;

investigate the earliest traceable source;

express the idea in SageVani's own words.

Translation discipline

A translation is an interpretation.

When a piece depends on one Sanskrit word:

1. inspect more than one translation;

2. check a lexicon;

3. examine the commentarial context when possible;

4. avoid building an essay on an English word that the Sanskrit does not cleanly support.

If two translations meaningfully diverge, that divergence may itself be worth exploring.

Sanskrit terminology

At first meaningful use:

vairāgya — commonly translated as dispassion or non-attachment

Then write naturally. Do not overload a paragraph with Sanskrit to make it sound profound.

Formal research notes should preserve standard diacritics when available: Ātman, Brahman, māyā, mokṣa, jñāna, bhakti, sādhanā, vairāgya, śraddhā.

Public-facing titles may omit diacritics if readability, search behavior, or platform limitations warrant it, but the article should still define the term carefully.

Myth, allegory, and symbolic interpretation

Before writing “X represents Y,” ask:

Does the primary text say this?

Does a named traditional commentator say this?

Is this a later devotional interpretation?

Is this a modern psychological reading?

Is this my own contemplative association?

Then phrase it accordingly.

Health, trauma, suffering, and spiritual bypassing

SageVani must never suggest that:

illness exists because someone failed spiritually;

trauma should simply be “surrendered” instead of treated;

grief demonstrates insufficient faith;

meditation, mantra, prayer, or yoga necessarily replaces professional care;

abuse should be endured as karma or dharma;

every tragedy is divinely sent for a lesson.

Spiritual practices may accompany care. They are not a rhetorical weapon against human suffering.

AI use

AI may assist with:

brainstorming questions;

locating possible sources;

comparing structure;

generating research checklists;

copyediting after the core thought exists.

AI should not be treated as a source for Sanskrit meanings, scripture quotations, textual history, or claims about a sampradāya. Those must be verified independently.

Most importantly: SageVani should not outsource its inward noticing.

Corrections policy

When an error is found:

1. correct the article;

2. note the correction when it materially changes the argument;

3. preserve humility rather than defensiveness;

4. update the internal Source Card so the mistake does not recur.

Being corrigible is part of the spiritual posture of the project.

Pre-publication integrity questions

Before publishing, ask:

1. Is it true to the best of my ability?

2. Can I source every factual or scriptural claim that needs sourcing?

3. Have I distinguished text from interpretation?

4. Have I distinguished one tradition's view from the whole of Sanātana Dharma?

5. Have I distinguished traditional symbolism from my own symbolism?

6. Is the piece useful rather than merely clever?

7. Am I spiritualizing away a human problem that deserves direct care or action?

8. Have I actually lived with this question long enough to say something honest?

9. Would I still publish this if it received no praise?

10. Does it return the reader to attention, practice, or inquiry?
